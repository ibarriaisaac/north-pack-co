Best GPS Devices for Hikers

Finding a reliable GPS device can mean the difference between a confident summit and an anxious backtrack. Whether you're day hiking well-marked trails or pushing into remote backcountry, the right device keeps you oriented, tracks your progress, and gives you a safety net when cell service disappears. This guide cuts straight to the best options available right now, with honest assessments of who each device is best suited for.

Garmin inReach Mini 2

The Garmin inReach Mini 2 Garmin inReach Mini 2 is the gold standard for hikers who want satellite communication without hauling a brick in their pack. It weighs just 100 grams and slips into a hip belt pocket or clips to a shoulder strap without a second thought.

Where this device shines is its two-way messaging over the Iridium satellite network, which covers the entire globe. You can send and receive texts, trigger an SOS with a dedicated button, and share your live track with people at home — all without cellular coverage. Navigation features are functional rather than flashy: you get basic waypointing, breadcrumb tracking, and turn-by-turn directions when paired with the Garmin Explore app on your phone.

What we like: The battery life stretches to 14 days at a standard tracking interval, and the device is rugged enough to meet military-grade durability standards (MIL-STD-810). The companion app is one of the more polished in the GPS space. What to watch: It requires a monthly satellite subscription plan (starting around $15/month for the basic plan), which adds to the total cost of ownership. The screen is small by design, so managing complex routes on the device itself is awkward. Best for: Solo hikers, international trekkers, and anyone who considers emergency communication non-negotiable.

Garmin GPSMAP 67i

If budget isn't your primary concern and you want the most complete handheld GPS hiking experience currently available, the Garmin GPSMAP 67i Garmin GPSMAP 67i delivers it. This is a full-featured GPS navigator with satellite messaging built in — the combination that puts it in its own category.

The 3-inch sunlight-readable display renders TopoActive maps clearly even on bright afternoons. Buttons are large and glove-friendly, which matters when temperatures drop and dexterity suffers. It locks onto GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou satellite systems, meaning it acquires position faster and holds it more reliably in deep canyons and dense tree cover than most competitors.

Like the Mini 2, it runs on the Iridium network for two-way messaging and SOS capability. The difference is that navigation is the primary function here, not a secondary feature bolted onto a communicator. You get detailed topographic maps preloaded, the ability to plan complex multi-day routes, and a wireless pairing system that lets you share routes and waypoints with other compatible Garmin devices nearby.

What we like: Battery life reaches 165 hours in GPS mode at the standard tracking interval. Preloaded maps mean you're not dependent on downloading before each trip. What to watch: It's heavy for a handheld at 230 grams, and the satellite subscription adds ongoing cost. Overkill for casual trail hikers. Best for: Expedition hikers, mountaineers, and anyone venturing into areas where both navigation precision and emergency communication are serious concerns.

Garmin eTrex 32x

The Garmin eTrex 32x Garmin eTrex 32x is the practical choice for hikers who want dependable GPS navigation without satellite messaging fees or a complicated feature set. It's been a trail staple for years because it simply works.

The 2.2-inch display isn't large, but it's readable, and the button-based interface is intuitive enough that you can operate it with gloves without swearing. It receives GPS and GLONASS signals, supports preloaded TopoActive maps, and runs on two AA batteries — a significant advantage for longer trips where you can carry spares and swap them anywhere on earth.

Route planning is straightforward. Load waypoints from the Garmin Connect or BaseCamp software before your hike, and the device guides you point to point. Wireless connectivity allows you to share tracks with other Garmin devices, which is useful when hiking with a group.

What we like: Two AA batteries mean zero charging anxiety. The interface is simple enough for GPS newcomers and the price point is reasonable for what you get. What to watch: No satellite messaging, no altimeter barometer (you need the 32x specifically for that feature over the 22x), and the screen can feel cramped when reading detailed maps. No touchscreen. Best for: Casual to intermediate hikers, beginners learning GPS navigation, and anyone who prefers simplicity and battery reliability over advanced features.

Garmin Montana 700i

The Garmin Montana 700i Garmin Montana 700i is purpose-built for hikers who cover serious off-trail ground and want a screen they can actually read without squinting. The 5-inch touchscreen is the largest in the handheld GPS category and responds well even with light gloves.

It combines the satellite messaging and SOS capability of the inReach system with comprehensive navigation tools on a platform rugged enough to handle drops, dust, and rain. You can download detailed topographic maps including BirdsEye satellite imagery, draw routes freehand on the screen, and manage large collections of waypoints from multi-day expeditions.

The size and weight — 400 grams with batteries — make it less appealing as a standalone carry device on a long day hike, but strapped to a pack or vehicle mount for approach drives, it performs exceptionally.

What we like: The large screen dramatically improves usability. Accepts both AA and rechargeable lithium battery packs for flexibility. What to watch: It's bulky and heavy for a hiking device, and the satellite subscription applies here too. Not a device you'll want clipped to your pack on a 20-mile ridge traverse. Best for: Off-trail explorers, hunters covering large areas, and hikers who prioritize screen size and map readability over packability.

Magellan eXplorist TRX7

The Magellan eXplorist TRX7 Magellan eXplorist TRX7 occupies an interesting niche. Originally designed with off-road motorsports in mind, it translates well for hikers who want a rugged touchscreen device with strong trail database access and don't need satellite messaging.

The 7-inch screen is extremely readable, it's preloaded with trail maps, and the interface is more intuitive for users accustomed to consumer touchscreen devices rather than button-based GPS units. It connects to the Magellan trail community for downloading user-generated routes.

What we like: Large touchscreen, solid trail database, and a different ecosystem than Garmin for hikers who prefer alternatives. What to watch: Heavier and larger than most dedicated hiking GPS units, no satellite messaging, and Magellan's map update ecosystem isn't as robust as Garmin's. Best for: Hikers who also mountain bike or use ATVs and want one device across activities.

Garmin inReach Messenger

The Garmin inReach Messenger Garmin inReach Messenger is the most affordable entry point into satellite communication for hikers. It's not primarily a navigation device — GPS tracking and location sharing are present, but route navigation is minimal. Its strength is keeping you connected and callable when everything else fails.

At roughly 115 grams and a compact form factor, it pairs well with a phone running the Garmin Explore app for full navigation while adding emergency communication capability at a price that makes it accessible.

What we like: Lower hardware cost than the Mini 2 with similar core safety features. A compelling safety upgrade for hikers currently relying solely on a smartphone. What to watch: Navigation features are intentionally limited. Best treated as a communication device with location tracking, not a standalone GPS navigator. Best for: Hikers on a tighter budget who want the safety net of satellite messaging without paying for navigation hardware they won't use.

How to Choose the Right GPS Device

Prioritize satellite messaging if you regularly hike solo, cover remote terrain, or venture internationally — the annual subscription cost is modest compared to the safety value. If you hike marked trails with occasional off-trail extensions, a dedicated GPS navigator like the eTrex 32x covers your needs without ongoing fees. Think honestly about screen size: a device you find frustrating to read in the field is one you'll stop using. Battery type matters on multi-day trips, and AA compatibility offers the most logistical flexibility when you're far from an outlet. Match the device to your actual hiking style rather than the most extreme trips you imagine taking — you'll carry it more consistently and use it more confidently when it fits the way you actually move through terrain.
