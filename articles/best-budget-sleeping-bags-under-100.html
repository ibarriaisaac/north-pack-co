Best Budget Sleeping Bags Under $100

Finding a solid sleeping bag without spending a fortune is genuinely possible — the market for sub-$100 options has improved dramatically over the last few years. This guide covers the best picks across different use cases, along with everything you need to know to choose the right one for how and where you actually sleep outside.

Teton Sports Celsius Regular

The Teton Sports Celsius Regular is the go-to recommendation for anyone who wants a reliable bag for casual, warm-weather camping without spending more than fifty dollars. It's rated to 0°C (32°F), uses a brushed, flannel-like lining that feels genuinely comfortable against skin, and stuffs into a compression sack that fits easily in the trunk of a car.

This is not a bag you'll want to carry on a multi-day backpacking trip — at over four pounds, the weight adds up fast. But for car camping, cabin trips, or keeping in a gear closet for guests, it punches well above its price point. The zipper runs smoothly, the hood cinches down effectively, and the exterior shell resists light moisture without soaking through.

What we like: Low price, comfortable lining, decent temperature rating for the cost What to know: Heavy for backpacking, synthetic fill compresses less than down Best for: Car campers, festival goers, beginners

Coleman North Rim

If you regularly camp in shoulder season — late fall, early spring, or at elevation — the Coleman North Rim is the most capable cold-weather bag under $100. Coleman rates it to -12°C (10°F), which is aggressive for this price tier. Real-world performance lands it more reliably around 20–25°F for average sleepers, but that still covers a wide range of conditions.

The bag uses a semi-rectangular cut that gives your legs room to move without the thermal inefficiency of a true rectangular bag. The draft tube behind the zipper is thick and well-sewn — a detail often skipped at this price. Insulation is synthetic and holds its warmth rating even when slightly damp, which matters if you're camping in variable weather.

At about five pounds, it's heavy, but Coleman has always prioritized warmth-per-dollar over packability at this tier. If car camping in cold conditions is your primary use case, nothing in this price range competes consistently.

What we like: Aggressive temperature rating, draft tube, moisture-tolerant synthetic fill What to know: Very heavy, not suitable for backpacking Best for: Cold-weather car campers, truck bed camping

REI Co-op Trailbreak 30

The REI Co-op Trailbreak 30 is the first bag on this list built around the needs of backpackers rather than car campers. At around two pounds, it packs down to roughly the size of a Nalgene bottle — manageable in a 40- or 50-liter pack. The 30°F rating holds reasonably well for average sleepers, and the mummy-style cut retains heat without feeling claustrophobic.

REI's house brand consistently delivers good quality control, and the Trailbreak 30 benefits from that. Stitching is clean, the zipper doesn't snag, and the hood sits snugly without needing constant readjustment throughout the night. For three-season backpacking at moderate elevations, this bag handles the full range of expected conditions.

It won't replace a down bag for serious alpine use or ultralight trips, but for someone building out a first backpacking kit on a budget, it's a smart, durable choice that will last several seasons with basic care.

What we like: Packable, reliable temperature rating, good build quality What to know: Synthetic, so heavier than down at this temperature rating Best for: Three-season backpackers, beginners building a kit

Kelty Cosmic 20

The Kelty Cosmic 20 is the most interesting option on this list because it's the only bag here using down insulation at under $100. Kelty uses 550-fill duck down, which isn't as efficient as the 700- or 800-fill goose down in premium bags, but it still provides a warmth-to-weight ratio that synthetic fill can't match at the same price.

Rated to 20°F, the Cosmic 20 is genuinely cold-worthy, and it compresses significantly better than any synthetic bag in this guide. The tradeoff is wet-weather performance — down loses insulating ability when wet, and budget down bags rarely include the hydrophobic treatments found in more expensive options. If you camp primarily in dry climates, this tradeoff is easy to accept. If you're regularly camping in the Pacific Northwest or the Appalachians in fall, synthetic is the safer choice.

The mummy shape is efficient, the draft collar works well, and the Kelty ThermaCapture lining helps reflect body heat back. For the price, the Cosmic 20 is genuinely impressive.

What we like: Real down insulation, excellent packability, 20°F rating What to know: Down loses warmth when wet, no hydrophobic treatment Best for: Dry-climate backpackers, campers who want down on a budget

Marmot Trestles Elite Eco 30

The Marmot Trestles Elite Eco 30 is built with recycled materials — recycled shell, lining, and synthetic fill — making it the right pick if sustainability factors into your purchasing decisions. Marmot doesn't sacrifice performance for the eco credentials, though. The bag performs well at its 30°F rating, uses a well-designed mummy cut, and includes a full-length zipper with a solid draft tube.

At right around the $99 mark, it sits at the top of this price range, but Marmot's construction quality justifies it. The bag's internal pocket is a nice touch for keeping a phone or headlamp accessible during the night. Packability is average for a synthetic bag at this temperature rating — you won't mistake it for a down bag, but it fits reasonably in a pack's sleeping bag compartment.

What we like: Recycled materials, quality construction, internal pocket What to know: At the high end of the budget range Best for: Eco-conscious buyers, three-season backpackers

ALPS Mountaineering Crescent Lake

The ALPS Mountaineering Crescent Lake is the pick for sleepers who feel cramped in mummy bags or for couples who want two bags that zip together. It's a wide rectangular design available in several temperature ratings, with the 20°F version typically landing under $90. The generous cut means you can sleep in any position, toss and turn freely, and layer inside the bag comfortably.

ALPS builds this bag to last — the shell fabric is durable, the zippers are heavy-duty, and the seams hold up through consistent use. It's a heavier bag, clearly designed for car camping, but the quality feel is several steps above basic discount-store options.

What we like: Wide cut, double-zip for connecting bags, durable construction What to know: Heavy and bulky, not for backpacking Best for: Car campers who sleep hot or want to connect bags

How to Choose the Right Budget Sleeping Bag

Before buying, answer two questions: where are you sleeping, and how cold will it get? Car campers can prioritize warmth and comfort over weight, which opens up heavier synthetic options like the Coleman North Rim or ALPS Crescent Lake. Backpackers need packability, making the REI Trailbreak 30, Kelty Cosmic 20, or Marmot Trestles Elite Eco 30 the practical choices. If you sleep cold, size down at least 10°F from the rated temperature on any bag you consider — manufacturer ratings assume average sleep metabolism and ideal conditions. For most three-season use between 30°F and 50°F nights, the REI Trailbreak 30 or Marmot Trestles Eco cover the widest range of people and conditions at a price that leaves room in the budget for other gear.
