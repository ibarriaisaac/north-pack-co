Best Ultralight Backpacking Tents

Cutting pack weight without sacrificing protection is the central challenge of ultralight backpacking, and your tent is usually the biggest opportunity. The options below have been tested across a range of conditions — from high-Sierra granite to rainy Pacific Northwest ridgelines — and represent the best balance of weight, livability, and durability available right now.

Big Agnes Copper Spur HV UL2

The Big Agnes Copper Spur HV UL2 is probably the most well-rounded ultralight two-person tent on the market. It weighs in around 2 lbs 6 oz in its standard configuration and sets up quickly with a color-coded hubbed pole system that even tired hikers can figure out at dusk.

What makes it stand out is the vertical wall geometry — "HV" stands for high-volume — which gives two occupants meaningfully more headroom and elbow space than most competitors at this weight. The interior has two doors, two vestibules, and multiple gear pockets, so it lives more like a 3-season tent than an ultralight one.

The ripstop nylon floor and fly hold up well across seasons. It's not the tent for a dedicated gram-counter, but it's the one that couples, friends, and hikers who occasionally go solo will keep coming back to. At this price point, durability and versatility are hard to beat.

Best for: Two-person trips, shoulder-season conditions, hikers who prioritize livability Weight: ~2 lbs 6 oz Packed size: 18 × 5 in

Zpacks Duplex

If weight is your primary filter, the Zpacks Duplex sets the bar. At approximately 1 lb 1 oz, it's a fully functional two-person freestanding shelter built from Dyneema Composite Fabric — the same material used in ultralight tarps and cuben fiber gear. That weight is almost absurd for a tent with two doors, two vestibules, and reasonable interior space.

The trade-offs are real, though. Dyneema doesn't stretch, which means seams can work under pressure differently than nylon. The tent also requires trekking poles for setup, which most ultralight hikers use anyway but is worth noting. It's less bombproof in sustained heavy wind than a rigid-pole tent and needs careful staking to perform well.

For experienced ultralight backpackers doing long-distance routes or fast-and-light weekend missions, the Duplex is in a category by itself. The price is steep, but the weight savings over 100+ miles add up fast.

Best for: Long-distance hikers, aggressive weight targets, experienced backpackers Weight: ~1 lb 1 oz Packed size: 6 × 4.5 in

Nemo Hornet OSMO 2P

The Nemo Hornet OSMO 2P hits a sweet spot that's easy to overlook: it's under $400, weighs around 2 lbs, and uses Nemo's OSMO fabric — a nylon/polyester blend that resists sagging when wet far better than plain nylon. That last point matters more than it sounds on a multi-day trip in variable weather.

The geometry is narrow, so two average-sized hikers will feel the walls at night, but solo use is genuinely comfortable. Setup is intuitive with a single pole-and-hub system, and the dual-door design means neither occupant has to crawl over the other. Vestibule space is modest but workable for boots and a pack.

If you're coming from a heavier 3-season tent and want to try ultralight without spending Zpacks money, this is the tent. It's also a legitimate choice for experienced backpackers who want a reliable secondary shelter.

Best for: First ultralight tent buyers, solo-to-duo flexible use, wet climates Weight: ~2 lbs Packed size: 18 × 4.5 in

MSR Carbon Reflex 1

The MSR Carbon Reflex 1 is a solo tent built for people who don't make compromises. Carbon fiber poles keep the weight at roughly 1 lb 14 oz while maintaining the kind of pole rigidity that performs in actual storm conditions. This is the tent you'd take into the mountains in early June when the weather is still unpredictable.

The single-wall design means no separate fly to deal with — the tent body itself handles precipitation. Condensation management is the usual trade-off with single-wall construction, and the Carbon Reflex handles it better than most through strategic venting, but you'll still notice moisture on cold nights. Paired with a bivy liner or a dry sleep system, it's a non-issue.

Vestibule space is limited, and the interior is cozy rather than spacious, but solo hikers doing technical routes will appreciate how taut and stable it stays under load. MSR's build quality is also among the best in the industry for long-term durability.

Best for: Solo fast-and-light hikers, alpine and technical terrain, stormy conditions Weight: ~1 lb 14 oz Packed size: 15 × 4.5 in

Six Moon Designs Lunar Solo

The Six Moon Designs Lunar Solo is where ultralight gets genuinely affordable. At around $200–$230 and weighing approximately 1 lb 2 oz, it's a silnylon trekking-pole shelter with a single-door design and just enough vestibule to keep your boots dry.

Setup requires one trekking pole and a learning curve — the geometry takes a few trips to dial in, and staking correctly makes a big difference in storm performance. Once you've got it down, though, it pitches fast and low, handles moderate rain well, and packs into almost nothing.

This is the tent for ultralight purists on a budget, solo thru-hikers doing established routes, and anyone who already uses trekking poles. It's not the choice for windy exposed ridgelines or conditions that demand a rigid structure, but for most 3-season use, it delivers far above its price.

Best for: Thru-hikers, budget ultralight, solo use with trekking poles Weight: ~1 lb 2 oz Packed size: 4 × 10 in

Tarptent Aeon Li

The Tarptent Aeon Li is a shelter that often gets overlooked because Tarptent doesn't have the marketing presence of the bigger brands. That's a mistake. At roughly 20 oz and priced around $300–$350, it offers freestanding performance with a trekking-pole assist and outstanding weather protection for a shelter at this weight.

The silpoly fabric resists sagging and stretching better than silnylon when wet, which keeps the pitch tight through a rainstorm. The single-door layout with a generous vestibule gives you real gear storage, and the interior has enough volume for a tall person to sit up comfortably. It's a genuinely complete shelter, not a stripped-down weight exercise.

For solo backpackers who spend time in wet or variable conditions and want a sub-$400 option that punches above its weight class, the Aeon Li deserves a serious look.

Best for: Solo use in wet conditions, foul-weather reliability, value-focused buyers Weight: ~20 oz Packed size: 5 × 12 in

How to Choose the Right Ultralight Tent

Before buying, narrow your decision with three questions: How many people need to sleep in it? What conditions will you typically encounter? And what's your actual weight target? A 2-person tent used solo wastes space and money; a trekking-pole shelter used by someone who carries ski poles creates unnecessary headaches. The Copper Spur HV UL2 and Hornet OSMO 2P are the safest picks for most backpackers because they handle a wide range of conditions without demanding perfect technique. If you're committed to sub-2 lb weight and have experience with non-freestanding shelters, the Zpacks Duplex or Lunar Solo will serve you well. For solo alpine and shoulder-season use, the MSR Carbon Reflex 1 and Tarptent Aeon Li offer weather confidence that the lighter options can't fully match. Match the shelter to your actual trip profile, not your aspirational one.
