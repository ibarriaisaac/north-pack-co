Down vs Synthetic Insulation: Which Sleeping Bag Fill Is Right for You

Choosing between down and synthetic insulation is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make when building a sleep system. Both materials keep you warm, but they behave differently under real trail conditions — and the wrong choice can leave you shivering or sweating through money on a bag that doesn't match how you actually camp.

What "Fill" Actually Means and Why It Matters

Sleeping bag insulation — the fill — is the material that traps warm air around your body. Down fill comes from the soft clusters beneath the feathers of ducks or geese. Synthetic fill is made from polyester fibers engineered to mimic down's loft. Every other performance characteristic — warmth-to-weight ratio, packability, moisture resistance, lifespan, and price — flows from that fundamental difference in material.

Fill power is the key metric for down. It measures how many cubic inches one ounce of down occupies. An 800-fill-power down bag is lighter and more compressible than a 600-fill bag at the same warmth rating, because the higher-quality clusters trap more air per unit of weight. Synthetic bags don't use fill power — instead, manufacturers describe insulation by fiber type, weight in grams, or proprietary names like PrimaLoft Gold or Polarguard.

The Case for Down: Warmth, Weight, and Longevity

Down is the benchmark for sleeping bag insulation for good reason. A quality down bag offers the best warmth-to-weight ratio available, compresses into a pack sack roughly the size of a football, and — if cared for properly — can last 15 to 20 years without significant loft loss. For backpackers counting grams and thru-hikers doing hundreds of trail nights, those advantages compound quickly.

The Western Mountaineering UltraLite Western Mountaineering UltraLite is the clearest example of what premium down can do. Filled with 850-fill-power goose down, it weighs just 1 lb 13 oz in the long version and packs to nearly nothing. It's made in the USA with a snug mummy cut, and serious alpine campers treat it like a long-term investment rather than a gear purchase.

For a slightly more accessible price point with nearly the same performance, the Feathered Friends Swift 20 Feathered Friends Swift 20 earns consistent praise from cold-weather backpackers. Its 900-fill-power VerticalDuck down is ethically sourced, and the bag's YKK zippers and refined baffle construction hold up through years of hard use.

Budget-minded backpackers who still want genuine down should look at the REI Co-op Magma 15 REI Co-op Magma 15. At around $300, it uses 850-fill-power DownTek water-resistant treated down and hits a 15°F comfort rating — a genuine three-season bag that punches well above its price.

Down's main vulnerability is moisture. Wet down collapses, loses loft, and stops insulating until it dries completely. Water-resistant down treatments (like DownTek or HyperDRY) significantly improve performance in light rain or condensation, but they are not a substitute for a dry sleeping environment. If you regularly camp in the Pacific Northwest, Southeast, or anywhere with sustained rain and humidity, take that honestly into account.

The Case for Synthetic: Reliability in Wet and Variable Conditions

Synthetic insulation doesn't match down's warmth-to-weight ratio, but it does something down cannot: it retains meaningful insulating ability even when wet. Polyester fibers don't absorb water the way down clusters do, so a damp synthetic bag still keeps you warmer than a damp down bag of equivalent dry-weather rating.

Synthetic bags are also significantly cheaper at every tier, dry faster, are easier to wash at home, and work well for people with down allergies. For casual car campers, humid-climate backpackers, paddlers, and anyone whose trips involve water crossings or prolonged wet weather, synthetic is often the more practical choice.

It uses recycled synthetic fill, packs reasonably well for its price, and handles moisture confidently. It won't impress ultralight obsessives, but it will keep you warm on a damp summer night without anxiety.

For a synthetic bag that actually competes with lower-end down on weight, look at Patagonia's 850 Down 32°F Patagonia 850 Down 32°F — note that despite the name, Patagonia uses Traceable Down in some versions and PrimaLoft synthetic in others depending on the current product run, so check the spec sheet. Best Ultralight Options for Backpackers

If your priority is minimizing pack weight without compromising warmth, this category is almost entirely down territory.

It uses 900-fill-power Nikwax Hydrophobic Down in an ultra-thin Pertex Quantum shell, and the whole package weighs around 1 lb 5 oz. The trade-off is a snug fit and less durability than heavier-shelled bags, so it's best suited for experienced backpackers who know their sleep systems.

For those who camp mostly in summer and early fall, the Marmot Hydrogen 30 Marmot Hydrogen 30 hits a sweet spot of light weight, packability, and realistic warmth for above-freezing nights. Its 800-fill-power down and Pertex Quantum shell deliver genuine ultralight performance without the price of the most elite options.

Best for Car Camping and Casual Use

Car campers operate under different constraints. Weight is largely irrelevant, pack size doesn't matter much, and the bag often lives in a storage closet for months between uses. Synthetic bags shine here because they tolerate compression storage better than down (which should always be stored uncompressed), they're machine washable without special care, and they cost less.

The Kelty Cosmic Down 20 Kelty Cosmic Down 20 offers entry-level down performance at a price that makes sense for weekend use. It's heavier than premium down bags and the fill power is lower, but it's well-made for the price and warmer than most synthetic bags in its class. A good gift option for someone just getting into camping.

Key Factors to Decide Between Down and Synthetic

Choose down if:
  • You backpack regularly and weight and packability matter
  • You camp in mostly dry climates or shoulder-season conditions
  • You're willing to invest in long-term gear
  • You'll store the bag properly (uncompressed, in a large storage sack)
Choose synthetic if:
  • You camp in wet, humid, or unpredictable conditions
  • You want a lower upfront cost
  • You need easy home washing and maintenance
  • You're buying for occasional or casual use
---

Buying Advice

Don't let the down-versus-synthetic debate distract you from temperature rating, which is the most important spec of all. A well-made synthetic bag rated to 20°F will keep you warmer than a premium down bag rated to 35°F — full stop. Narrow down your typical conditions and required temperature rating first, then use fill type to optimize weight, packability, and price within that constraint. If you camp in one predictable environment, the right fill type becomes obvious. If you camp across a range of conditions, consider owning one of each rather than compromising on a single bag that does neither job perfectly.
